\subsection*{Part B: Ensemble geometry}

To assess the reliability of geodesic versus Euclidean distances, we train $M=10$ VAEs for each ensemble size (1, 2, and 3 decoders) and compute the coefficient of variation (CoV) across models for each of 25 fixed test-point pairs. For a fixed pair $(y_i, y_j)$, the CoV is

\begin{equation}
    \text{CoV}_{ij} = \frac{\sigma\{d_{ij}^{(1)}, \ldots, d_{ij}^{(M)}\}}{\mu\{d_{ij}^{(1)}, \ldots, d_{ij}^{(M)}\}},
    \label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d_{ij}^{(m)}$ is the distance (Euclidean or geodesic) between the latent means of $y_i$ and $y_j$ under the $m$th VAE, and $\sigma$ denotes the sample standard deviation. Geodesics are computed by minimising the model-average energy from \autoref{eq:cov}, approximating the expectation over decoder pairs $(l,k)$ with Monte Carlo (see code snippets).

\autoref{fig:part_b} shows the ensemble latent space and the mean per-pair CoV as a function of decoder count. The Euclidean CoV remains roughly constant at $0.190$, $0.196$, and $0.214$ for 1, 2, and 3 decoders respectively -- basically: ensemble size has no effect on Euclidean stability, as expected since those distances do not involve the decoder. In contrast, the geodesic CoV drops from $0.142$ with one decoder to $0.065$ with two and $0.051$ with three, a reduction of roughly $64\,\%$. The largest gain comes from adding just a second decoder, with a further modest improvement at three. With three decoders, geodesic distances are roughly four times more stable than Euclidean distances across retrainings. The model-average energy achieves this by penalising curve variance across the ensemble -- paths through regions where decoders disagree carry high energy and are avoided, drawing geodesics toward the data manifold where models are in agreement. Where a single decoder is noisy, the ensemble average is not.
